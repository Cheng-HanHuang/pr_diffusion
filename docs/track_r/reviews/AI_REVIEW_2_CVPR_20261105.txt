    Best-of-K Without Ground Truth: Selection,
    Stopping and Unsolved Images in Diffusion
    Phase Retrieval
        CVPR                Submitted: November 5, 2026




             Summary
       The paper investigates how to select and when to stop multiple diffusion-prior
       phase-retrieval runs without access to ground truth, a pervasive gap between
       current reporting practice and real-world use. It proposes a simple, ground-truth-
       free protocol that (i) selects the run with the smallest Fourier-magnitude residual,
       (ii) applies a zero-shot orientation test to fix 180° twin ambiguities, and (iii) stops
       sampling per image once M runs meet a solver-specific residual threshold. Across
       FFHQ and ImageNet with four solvers (DAPS, SITCOM, FAST-DIPS, PC-DAPS), the
       protocol nearly matches an orientation-aware ground-truth oracle on FFHQ,
       improves over per-metric oracles on ImageNet in some settings, offers principled
       abstention flags, and includes a predictive model with preregistered forecasts
       demonstrating that selection is predictable while generation coverage (whether
       any run is good) is fundamentally limited.
 Strengths
Technical novelty and innovation

• Positions selection and stopping as first-class components of diffusion
    phase retrieval, formalizes when stopping helps, and proves a useful
    result (Proposition 1) linking expected compute, consistency rate, and
    success.

• Introduces a practically effective, zero-shot orientation correction and
    shows why orientation choice is a prior decision not visible to the
    likelihood.

• Moves beyond exchangeable best-of-n models by fitting a residual-race
    (Plackett–Luce) model that better reflects empirical selection statistics and
    enables preregistered forecasts.

• Provides a solver-agnostic residual threshold calibration strategy
    (including a label-free proxy), and a protocol with abstention flags that
    adheres to test-time constraints.

Experimental rigor and validation

• Comprehensive cross-solver, cross-dataset evaluation (DAPS-2K/4K,
    SITCOM, FAST-DIPS, PC-DAPS; FFHQ, ImageNet, and a held-out
    ImageNet’).

• Careful decomposition of performance into selection quality, orientation
    correction, and coverage (existence of a good run), with prospective
    registered tests that validate selection predictability.

• Strong transfer to particle-based PC-DAPS without extra compute,
    demonstrating generality beyond independent-run samplers.

• Transparent analysis of failure regimes: quantifies unsolved images,
    clarifies why consistent-yet-wrong runs defeat stopping, and shows limits
    of inference scaling with few runs.

Clarity of presentation

• Clearly explains the twin ambiguity and why measurement-based
    selection cannot resolve it, motivating the need for an external
    orientation prior/test.

• Distinguishes between selection and coverage; uses precise labels
    (GOOD, ORBIT, CONSISTENT, INCONSISTENT) and defines thresholds and
    policies unambiguously.

• States recommendations and limitations explicitly, with careful caveats
    around in-sample development panels versus frozen, held-out tests.

Significance of contributions

• Addresses a real gap in current practice: published “best-of-K with
    ground truth” is unavailable to users; the proposed protocol is actionable
    and measurable.

• Offers a principled reporting alternative and provides evidence that a
    large fraction of published gains remain available at test-time, improving
    the field’s evaluation standards.

• The predictability analysis and preregistration are exemplary for this
    literature, likely to influence future work on best-of-n sampling and
    verification.




 Weaknesses
Technical limitations or concerns

• The method relies on a solver- and noise-level-specific residual threshold
    τS that requires calibration (albeit with a label-free proxy); generalization
    across operators/noise models remains limited.

• Orientation correction via generic CLIP prompts is imperfect on
    heterogeneous datasets (ImageNet), leaving a nontrivial gap to the orbit
    oracle at higher K and in some panels.

• Results and the stopping theory hinge on independence/exchangeability
    assumptions across runs; solver dynamics could induce correlations not
    fully explored.

Experimental gaps or methodological issues

• While the paper compares multiple solvers, it does not include recent
    flow-based phase-retrieval pipelines (e.g., FlowSGS, FlowFPR) that also
    emphasize residual control and could interact with the proposed
    selection/stopping; this limits the breadth of empirical context.
    Sensitivity analyses are mostly around τS and budgets; there is less
• exploration of alternative verifiers (e.g., robust residuals, multi-view
    consistency, learned likelihood proxies) or other orientation scorers
    beyond CLIP and heuristic denoising-loss.

• Some compute comparisons consider equal number of runs, although
    solvers have different per-run costs; more systematic wall-clock or NFE-
    normalized analyses would strengthen conclusions about resource trade-
    offs.

Clarity or presentation issues

• The many cohorts/panels, thresholds, and flags can overwhelm readers; a
    compact main-text ablation figure clarifying where each component helps
    most would aid accessibility.

• Some proofs and estimator details are referenced to the supplement;
    brief sketch proofs or intuitions in the main text would help.

Missing related work or comparisons

• Limited discussion of closely related “residual-guided” or “verifier-
    dependent” stopping/selection in current flow/diffusion PR systems (e.g.,
    FlowFPR’s residual control, FlowSGS’s likelihood steps) and how the
    proposed protocol could plug in.

• The paper could more directly relate its exchangeable vs. race models to
    recent theory on best-of-n tail competition and verifier-imperfection
    limits, and discuss implications for designing better verifiers.




 Detailed Comments
Technical soundness evaluation

• The argument that selection by residual is effective within an image is
    well-supported: difference-of-residuals analysis cancels shared noise
    energy, explaining strong within-image ranking power.

• Proposition 1 is sound and provides actionable insights: expected runs,
    flag rates, and success relate cleanly to the consistency rate s and the
    class-conditional success probabilities Hj±. The observation that stopping
    helps only when inconsistency predicts incorrectness is theoretically and
    empirically compelling.

• The posterior-orbit argument is correct: orientation selection must come
    from the prior; the likelihood is invariant. Using zero-shot CLIP is a
    pragmatic instantiation of that Bayes-optimality idea (under a prior
    preference for upright images).

• The residual-race model (Plackett–Luce) better matches data than
    exchangeability and is a reasonable choice; preregistered forecasts
    bolster validity. The paper is also candid about model misspecification in
    generation coverage.

Experimental evaluation assessment

• Methodology is strong: shared measurements, multiple solvers, multiple
    budgets K/Nmax, and held-out ImageNet’ increase robustness. The
    arithmetic of subset-averaging for exact best-of-K estimators is
    appropriate.

• The registered prospective tests are a high bar; they convincingly show
    selection predictability and identify the main miss as generation coverage
    (unsolved images), not selection instability.

• On FFHQ, E1 nearly saturates orbit-oracle performance; on ImageNet, the
    protocol beats per-metric oracles at small K for DAPS due to orientation
    correction—this is a key and credible finding given that per-metric
    oracles are not orientation-aware.

• The PC-DAPS results are particularly useful: the proposed selection yields
    sizable gains “for free,” showing direct practical value and cross-method
    applicability.

• Limitations are transparently reported: the protocol can underperform
    fixed best-of-K at large budgets when many images never pass
    consistency, and FLAGC marks “hard” but not necessarily “unsolved.”

Comparison with related work (using the summaries provided)

• DPS and DAPS established diffusion-prior posterior sampling for inverse
    problems;        this   paper   is   orthogonal,   focusing   on   test-time
    selection/stopping and evaluation realism rather than a new sampler.

• PC-DAPS is included and benefits from the proposed post-hoc selection,
    suggesting complementarity: moving selection inside the sampler (PC-
    DAPS) and outside (this work) can be synergistic.
        FlowFPR and FlowSGS include residual/likelihood control inside their
   • alternations; the current paper does not empirically compare with these
        families. However, the selection/stopping protocol and reporting
        recommendations seem largely orthogonal and could be adopted to
        evaluate/operate such methods under realistic constraints.

   • Broader literature on verifiers and best-of-n (resampling limits, tail
        competition) is appropriately cited and conceptually integrated; the
        residual-race model extends beyond the exchangeable baseline used in
        prior analysis.

   Discussion of broader impact and significance

   • The protocol and reporting recommendations are immediately actionable
        and can correct misleading practices that hide failure modes (e.g., per-
        metric oracles, unreported unsolved fractions).

   • The formalization of where stopping helps and the identification of
        unsolved-image regimes provide a roadmap for future research: invest in
        better generation (samplers/priors) and better orientation priors/tests
        rather than expecting selection to “rescue” fundamentally ill-posed
        instances.

   • The preregistration practice is exemplary for the community; encouraging
        more predictive, model-based evaluation could reduce overfitting to
        specific panels and improve scientific rigor.




    Questions for Authors
1. How sensitive are the protocol’s gains to the exact definition of “GOOD” (PSNR
   thresholds 25/20 dB)? Could you provide sensitivity curves or show that
   relative conclusions (vs fixed best-of-K and oracles) persist across a range of
   thresholds?

2. Have you tried alternative residual-based verifiers (e.g., robust residuals, per-
   frequency weighting, or multiscale residuals) and did they change the balance
   between CONSISTENT wrong runs and GOOD runs, particularly for SITCOM
   and FAST-DIPS on ImageNet?
3. For orientation scoring, beyond CLIP prompts and the denoising-loss heuristic,
   could a learned, prior-aligned binary classifier trained only once (no ground-
   truth orientations per image) further reduce the gap on ImageNet while
   preserving the ground-truth-free constraint?

4. Can you report wall-clock or NFE-normalized comparisons at equal compute
   budgets across solvers to complement “equal number of runs” plots, especially
   for scenarios where stopping reallocates compute across images?

5. How robust is the label-free τS estimation to pilot size (e.g., 2 vs 4 runs) and to
   distribution shifts in noise or content? Would an online adaptive τS (e.g., per-
   batch robust estimate) be viable without materially increasing false positives?

6. Did you observe correlations across runs (e.g., seed-dependent mode
   attraction) that might violate independence assumptions in Proposition 1 or
   the race model? If so, how sensitive are the predictions/intervals to such
   correlations?

7. Could you comment on applying the protocol to other ambiguous operators
   (e.g., coded diffraction without twin ambiguity, or inline holography) and to
   other tasks (inpainting/super-resolution), where different symmetries and
   verifier behavior may arise?




    Overall Assessment
This paper tackles an important, under-addressed practical problem in diffusion-
based phase retrieval: how to select among multiple stochastic runs and when to
stop without ground truth. The proposed protocol is simple, sound, and effective;
the empirical evaluation is broad across solvers and datasets; and the modeling
and preregistered forecasts are exemplary. The work is not about a new sampler
but about making evaluation and deployment realistic and principled, and it
convincingly shows that most of the reported best-of-K gains are attainable at
test time with careful selection/orientation and judicious stopping. Limitations are
candidly discussed, notably imperfect orientation on heterogeneous data and
regimes where stopping can hurt at large budgets due to unsolved images. The
missing comparisons to recent flow-based PR pipelines are a gap but do not
undermine the central contributions, which are largely orthogonal and likely
beneficial to that line of work as well. Overall, this is a valuable and timely
contribution with clear impact on how the community reports and uses diffusion-
prior solvers for phase retrieval.
