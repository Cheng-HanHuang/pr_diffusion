    Best-of-K Without Ground Truth: Selection,
    Stopping and Unsolved Images in Diffusion
    Phase Retrieval
        ICLR            Submitted: November 5, 2026




             Summary
       This paper investigates how to select, stop, and report results in diffusion-prior
       Fourier phase retrieval without access to ground truth. It proposes a simple,
       ground-truth-free procedure that selects the run with the smallest measurement
       residual and corrects its orientation via a zero-shot test, and introduces a
       stopping rule that draws runs until M consistent ones are observed. Across DAPS,
       SITCOM, FAST-DIPS, and PC-DAPS on FFHQ and ImageNet, the authors show that
       this protocol nearly matches an orientation-aware oracle on FFHQ, can
       outperform the commonly used per-metric ground-truth oracle on ImageNet (due
       to orientation correction), improves particle selection for PC-DAPS, and offers
       predictive models and registered forecasts to understand best-of-K behavior and
       compute–accuracy tradeoffs.
 Strengths
Technical novelty and innovation

• The paper reframes selection and stopping for diffusion-based phase
    retrieval as a ground-truth-free problem, proposing a simple, universal
    protocol (residual pick plus zero-shot orientation correction) and a
    principled    stopping   rule    that   leverages   solver-calibrated   residual
    thresholds.

• Introduces a residual-race (Plackett–Luce) model that departs from
    exchangeable-ranks assumptions to predict how best-of-K scales, with
    exact closed-form estimators for averaging over subsets and six
    prospective, registered tests.

• Clear articulation of inherent limits: measurement residuals cannot certify
    correctness and cannot detect orbit symmetries; stopping only helps
    when failed runs are inconsistent with the measurement; a few runs
    cannot expose “unsolved” images due to mixture identifiability limits.

Experimental rigor and validation

• Broad, careful evaluation spanning four solvers (DAPS, SITCOM, FAST-
    DIPS, PC-DAPS), two datasets (FFHQ, ImageNet, plus a held-out
    ImageNet’), and multiple budgets (K up to 64); reports bootstrap
    intervals, prospective forecasts, and exact subset estimators; freezes the
    protocol and hypotheses before held-out scoring.

• Decomposes gaps into selection vs orientation contributions; analyzes
    per-class residual ranking; quantifies calibration and robustness of solver-
    specific residual thresholds, including label-free estimation.

• Demonstrates transfer to PC-DAPS particle selection, improving PSNR
    and fraction of GOOD images with no additional computation.

Clarity of presentation

• Clear problem setup (operator, symmetries, labeling), rigorous definitions
    (GOOD/ORBIT/CONSISTENT), and precise policies and oracles; helpful
    propositions and reasoning about stopping and identifiability.

• Thorough accounting of when and why the protocol helps or hurts, and a
    balanced, transparent treatment of failure cases and limits.

Significance of contributions
    Addresses a pervasive gap between published best-of-K-with-ground-
• truth practice and what users can deploy online; provides a concrete,
    effective, and reproducible alternative.

• Offers actionable recommendations for reporting and benchmarking,
    likely to improve rigor and comparability in the community.

• The predictive modeling and registered forecasts offer a rare level of
    methodological maturity in this space, applicable beyond phase retrieval.




 Weaknesses
Technical limitations or concerns

• Orientation correction hinges on prompts (CLIP) or heuristics and can be
    imperfect on generic scenes; results suggest nontrivial orientation
    residual gaps on ImageNet and ImageNet’, indicating that a stronger,
    learned orientation prior or scorer may be needed for robustness.

• The stopping analysis assumes independent, identically distributed runs
    and independence of pass events; interactions in practice (e.g., warm
    starts, adaptive hyperparameters) or solver correlations could violate
    these assumptions.

• Residual thresholds τS require per-solver, per-noise-level calibration;
    while a label-free proxy is provided, generalization to other noise models
    and operators remains untested.

Experimental gaps or methodological issues

• The “GOOD” label relies on PSNR thresholds (25 dB FFHQ, 20 dB
    ImageNet); although pragmatic, the choice is somewhat arbitrary and
    may not fully capture perceptual fidelity or task requirements.

• Limited exploration beyond the standard oversampled Fourier magnitude
    operator and mainly at σ=0.05 (aside from FAST-DIPS ablations), leaving
    generality   to   other   regimes    (e.g.,   coded   diffraction,   different
    oversampling factors, real experimental data) mostly untested.

• Some FFHQ images come from the training split for the denoiser (cohort
    B), which could bias results; although cohorts are reported separately, the
    consequence on absolute and comparative outcomes is not deeply
    analyzed.

Clarity or presentation issues

• The paper is dense and occasionally assumes substantial familiarity with
    recent solvers and selection protocols; clearer, consolidated baselines for
    the “per-metric oracle” vs. “orbit oracle” vs. “E0/E1” policies would help
    new readers align.

• Minor formatting artifacts from PDF extraction can make parts of tables
    and figure references harder to parse without the supplement.

Missing related work or comparisons

• Recent flow-based inverse samplers and phase retrieval methods (e.g.,
    FlowSGS, FlowFPR, DDRM-PR) that also rely on multi-restart or ground-
    truth-assisted selection are not discussed; they face similar evaluation
    pitfalls and could further contextualize the significance of ground-truth-
    free protocols.

• Broader literature on adaptive sampling/selection and particle guidance
    (e.g., interacting particle guidance, sequential allocation) could be more
    fully connected to the stopping and predictability analyses.




 Detailed Comments
Technical soundness evaluation

• The argument that residuals compare runs sharply within an image
    (shared noise realization) is convincing and supported empirically (e.g.,
    200/205 correct-vs-consistent orderings); the caution that residual
    thresholds cannot certify correctness is correct and well-illustrated across
    solvers.

• Proposition 1 provides a clean, interpretable decomposition of expected
    runs and success probability under a simple Bernoulli pass model; the
    insight that stopping helps only when measurement consistency predicts
    correctness (i.e., when failed runs tend to be inconsistent) is both intuitive
    and practically important.
    The residual-race model (Plackett–Luce weights) is a meaningful
• departure from exchangeable ranks, matches observed selection behavior
    better, and has good prospective calibration on the selected run. The
    demonstrated over-optimism in generation coverage (whether any run is
    good) is candid and informative; the identifiability argument (only first
    few moments learned from small K) explains why.

• The exact subset estimators for best-of-K are well-known but often
    neglected in empirical work; their inclusion strengthens the claims and
    reduces post hoc selection bias.

Experimental evaluation assessment

• The evaluation breadth is a major strength: multiple solvers, datasets,
    budgets, and orientation tests; the held-out, frozen protocol and
    registered forecasts add credibility.

• The improvements over fixed best-of-K at equal mean runs are significant
    where expected (DAPS configs on FFHQ), and neutral/negative where
    failed runs are largely consistent—this is precisely the behavior implied
    by the theory, lending coherence to the story.

• The PC-DAPS particle-selection results are particularly compelling: they
    deliver measurable quality gains at no extra cost and underscore the
    generality of the selection idea.

• Remaining concerns include the reliance on PSNR thresholds to define
    GOOD, modest exploration of other operators/noise levels, and limited
    insights into real-world data or non-synthetic noise mismatches.

Comparison with related work (using the summaries provided)

• DPS and DAPS initiated the current best-of-K-with-ground-truth practice
    that this paper critically revisits; SITCOM and FAST-DIPS reference code
    also use ground-truth selection, consistent with the authors’ claims.

• PC-DAPS (concurrent) moves selection inside the sampler via prior-
    consistency pruning, but still benefits from the proposed ground-truth-
    free selection among its particles; this complements PC-DAPS by showing
    an external, general selector can further improve outcomes.

• DPnP (plug-and-play diffusion for nonlinear inverse problems), FlowSGS
    (flow-matching with Split Gibbs Sampling), FlowFPR (Flow + RAAR), and
    DDRM-PR (DDRM + HIO) all demonstrate strong inverse performance but
    still report multi-restart/ground-truth-assisted selection; this paper’s
        protocol and reporting recommendations would address their evaluation
        gaps and make results more deployable.

   • Interacting particle guidance and sequential allocation literatures (e.g.,
        SMC/IPG, adaptive R&S) provide a theoretical backdrop for the stopping
        and particle interactions; while the paper cites some verifier-scaling and
        best-of-n works, tying the stopping rule more explicitly to those
        frameworks could further strengthen the positioning.

   Discussion of broader impact and significance

   • The work directly addresses a reproducibility and deployment gap:
        reported best-of-K-with-ground-truth performance is not available at test
        time. The proposed protocol is simple, solver-agnostic, and empirically
        effective, and the reporting recommendations, if adopted, would make
        published results more honest and actionable.

   • The analysis clarifies when best-of-K compute is better spent adaptively
        vs uniformly, and when orientation priors (or tests) dominate the
        residual-based verifier; these insights generalize to other ill-posed inverse
        problems with symmetries.

   • A key takeaway for the community is to report flags and unsolved
        coverage, not just aggregated best-of-K numbers; this shifts focus to
        genuine solver progress instead of improved selection only.




    Questions for Authors
1. How sensitive is the protocol to the choice of M (e.g., M=2 vs 3 or confidence-
   conditioned stopping)? Could a simple adaptive scheme (e.g., stop when two
   consistent runs agree on orientation with margin) improve ImageNet’
   performance without hurting FFHQ?

2. For orientation, did you try a learned, paired preference model (e.g.,
   contrastive classifier trained on upright vs rotated orbit pairs) instead of CLIP
   prompts or denoising-loss heuristics? How would such a scorer generalize
   across datasets and classes?

3. The label-free τS estimation uses min-residual pilots from four runs per image.
   How robust is this to heavy-tailed residual distributions or heterogeneous
   image difficulty, especially for solvers that overfit measurement noise?

4. Can you provide per-solver wall-clock compute comparisons for the protocol
   vs fixed best-of-K at the same mean runs, including any overhead from
   orientation scoring, to contextualize the practical runtime benefits?

5. The independence assumptions for pass events and residual ranks underpin
   Proposition 1 and the race model. Did you check for run-to-run correlations
   within images (e.g., seeds that cause near-duplicates)? If present, how would
   this affect stopping gains and predictive coverage?

6. Could you expand on the failure cases where wrong-but-consistent runs
   dominate (e.g., SITCOM)? Are there measurable residual-based features (e.g.,
   spectral artifacts) that might identify such failure modes beyond τS?

7. The identifiability argument shows a few runs cannot reveal the mass near zero
   success. Practically, do you recommend a minimum per-image budget to
   characterize unsolved coverage (e.g., K≥32), and how would you report
   uncertainty on the “unsolved fraction” with small K?




    Overall Assessment

 Estimated Score: 7.1/10 (Calibrated to ICLR scale)


This paper tackles a timely and important problem: closing the gap between
ground-truth-assisted best-of-K reporting and what users can actually do at test
time in diffusion-based phase retrieval. The proposed residual-based selection
with a zero-shot orientation test and a simple stopping rule is principled, practical,
and well-supported by extensive experiments and prospective forecasts. The work
is methodologically careful, transparent about limits, and offers strong, actionable
reporting recommendations that could improve rigor across the field. While the
core ideas are conceptually simple, the contribution lies in establishing their
effectiveness, boundaries, and predictability across multiple modern solvers and
datasets, and in articulating exactly when stopping helps and when it cannot.
Some limitations remain—orientation scoring on generic scenes, reliance on PSNR
thresholds for GOOD, modest exploration of other operators/noises, and per-
solver calibration—but overall the paper is a valuable, community-facing
contribution. I recommend acceptance.
